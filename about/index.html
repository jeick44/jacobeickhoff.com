How I got here.

I am a Finance and Accounting student at the University of Wisconsin–Eau Claire, graduating in May 2027. Since May 2026 I have been embedded with the credit team at a community bank in the Upper Midwest, first as a summer analyst and now remotely, and the work has had two halves from the first week: I do the standard credit analyst work — spreading financials, writing risk assessments — and I build the tooling that changes how that work gets done.

The second half was not what I was hired for. It started because the process nobody had written down turned out to be the thing slowing everybody else down, and the fastest way to describe it was to build something that did it properly. That is the pattern the whole site is about: go into an undocumented process, find where it actually breaks, and close the gap with a tool built under constraints somebody else set. The constraints are the interesting part. At the Bank nothing may reach a cloud service and nothing may require a server, which rules out most of what a developer would reach for first and rules in a much smaller, much more deliberate set of choices.

The rest of my time goes to independent research — equity analysis, a portfolio framework, and a faculty-advised study on whether AI-generated language in filings predicts anything — and to Division III track and field. Those run at the same time as a full course load, not instead of it.

Why every Case Study here reports a problem.

Every Case Study on this site carries a What I Learned section, and it is never decorative: a defect I found in my own work, an assumption that turned out to be wrong, a perspective that changed. That is deliberate, and it is universal rather than selective, because work that never reported a problem is the work to trust least — either nothing was checked, or something was found and quietly left out. Reporting the problem is how I check myself, and it is the part of a record that is hardest to fake. It is also the part an interviewer can actually use.
