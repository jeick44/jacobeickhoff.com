Reading which companies use AI, not which ones talk about it

Four of us built a model together for Georgia State University’s Southeastern Hedge Fund Competition and submitted a formal paper as our deliverable. The model scored how far each S&P 500 company had put AI to work inside its own business, based on its earnings calls and filings, using only documents through the end of 2025. Over the first eight weeks of 2026, the top quartile, the quarter of companies it ranked highest, beat the bottom quartile by 11.4%.

The Situation

A hedge fund case competition asks for a formal paper with an investable idea and enough evidence behind it that somebody can argue against it. Early in 2026, nearly every one of those ideas was a bet on who sells AI, meaning the chips, the models and the cloud they run on, while far fewer were bets on who had actually put AI to work inside their business.

That gap stays open because the evidence is hard to read. Companies say a great deal about AI in their filings and on their earnings calls, and most of it is for positioning, so we asked whether the documents alone could tell a company running AI in its operations apart from a company with a paragraph about AI, and then whether the market pays for that difference.

Why the paper looked at companies using AI rather than selling it

The gains for companies selling AI show up as sales right away, while the gains for companies using it show up much later, as lower costs mixed in with everything else on the income statement.

That delay is where an outside investor can still act, because once a user’s savings reach its margins everybody can see them, but its earnings calls and filings describe the work first. What Changed shows how that argument held up in the numbers.

What I Did

We built the model as a team and shared responsibility for every part of the submission, from how each sentence was scored to the final score for each company. We split the paper into pieces and passed them around, so beyond the model I also worked on the financial modeling, the index construction and the presentation, and I co-wrote the final submission. Every piece, the model included, was read by someone who had not written it. My three teammates are not named here because they have not approved a public write-up.

The team’s pipeline reads every earnings call since 2024 and every SEC filing since 2023 for 487 S&P 500 companies, and pulls out each sentence that ties AI to a financial result, such as a cost saved, a margin improved or work done with fewer people. It sorts each one into one of 12 operational categories, and then Claude Opus, Anthropic’s large language model, scores each sentence from 0 to 6, where a 0 is buzzwords and a 6 is a CFO citing dollar figures across several deployments.

The score weighs four things:

  • Whether a company uses AI or sells it
  • How often it puts a number on the result
  • How hard analysts question its AI claims
  • Whether its AI talk is speeding up

These are joined with Bloomberg figures on margins, SG&A (overhead costs) and operating leverage (how much profit grows as sales grow). An ensemble, four machine-learning models whose outputs are combined, turns them into one score between 0 and 1 for each company.

The ensemble learned from 34 labeled companies: 20 AI winners named by Goldman Sachs and 14 software companies UBS expects AI to hurt. Both banks published those lists from their own research, so the labels came from outside the team.

One of the most important things we did before creating any deliverable was read through every prior year’s winning entry and take note of what the judges actually rewarded.

Reading the prior winners before writing a word

The entries that had won in earlier years did not have the strongest backtests, but their thesis fit in one sentence, and a judge could check their evidence in the ten minutes a judge actually has with each paper.

So we produced fewer exhibits, each able to stand on its own, and every figure in the abstract states the period it was measured over. This was one of the most transferable things I learned from the project: finding out what an audience rewards before spending a large amount of time producing for it.

How the four things are measured, and the cap on AI sellers

Using or selling. “AI cut our cost per package 8%” is a company using AI. “Our AI platform serves enterprise clients” is a company selling it. A company whose AI sentences are more than 70% the selling kind has its score capped at 0.35 out of 1. That keeps chipmakers and cloud providers, whose prices already reflect the enthusiasm, out of the top of the ranking.

Numbers. Sentences with a dollar or percentage result count for more than general claims of efficiency.

Analyst pressure. Each earnings call is split into management’s prepared remarks and the analysts’ Q&A. A wide gap between management’s AI claims and how hard analysts follow up on them suggests promotion.

Speed-up. The pipeline tracks whether a company’s talk about AI is growing faster quarter over quarter. That picks out companies early in deployment, before the savings reach their margins.

Limits. Banks, insurers and REITs (real estate investment trusts) are scored on their documents alone, because the usual margin measures do not fit how they make money. The ensemble learned from only 34 labeled companies, so the paper’s main evidence is the returns that came after scoring.

What Changed

The model was built only from documents through the end of 2025, which means every 2026 return was out of sample, a period the model never saw while it was being built. Across the 487 companies, the top quartile returned 9.9% from the start of January to February 27, 2026, while the bottom quartile lost 1.5%, a spread of 11.4%. The equal-weight S&P 500, where every company counts the same, returned 3.5% over the same weeks.

The pattern held across the whole range of scores, not only at the ends, with a correlation of +0.296 between a company’s score and its 2026 return.

Run on earlier years, the same score pointed the other way, with the top quartile trailing the bottom by 18.4% in 2024 and by 11.9% in 2025. We made the decision to report both years in full because they added more insight to the paper than they took away.

Companies that put AI to work in 2022 and 2023 had already started being rewarded by the market before our documents began, and with their deployments finished, they said little about AI in 2024 and 2025, so they scored low while their stock did well. We describe those two years as evidence of the lag. By 2026, a new group was in the middle of deploying, describing AI in their operations in specific terms before the savings reached their earnings, and that is the group the score ranks highest.

One of the primary checks of the model was the 453 of the 487 companies that never had a label. On those companies, the ones the model rated Buy returned 9.2%, against a 6.6% average for all 453, which shows the score is finding a pattern across the index and not just memorizing the 34 companies it learned from.

Second, a portfolio holding Goldman’s AI winners and shorting UBS’s likely losers went from −50.4% in 2024 to +13.9% in 2025 to +32.6% in early 2026, the same arc as the score. The paper is plain that eight weeks is a short live window, and a full year of 2026 returns is the real test.

This model is separate from the pipeline I later worked on in The Research, since it measures a different thing in different documents and was built months before.

What I Learned

The first lesson was to report every result, including the ones that went against you, because the full picture gives the reader more information and more insight into what the data is actually showing. The score lost in two of the three years it was tested on, and the paper shows all three.

Why the losing years went into the paper

Leaving 2024 and 2025 out would have made a cleaner paper, but a judge who found the missing years on their own would have seen a large hole. A judge who is handed those years with an explanation is reading a paper that already knows where it is weakest.

The losing years are also what any explanation of the result has to account for, and without them, the 2026 result would have been one good window with nothing to test the lag against.

The second lesson is about how a team checks its own work. Because every piece was passed around, nobody was the last reader of their own section. That mattered most for the model, because the people who built it were the ones least able to see its gaps.

Where Else This Applies

Reporting the periods that went against you, and explaining them. Any performance claim is measured over some window, whether for a strategy, a pricing change, a marketing channel or a process redesign. That window is usually chosen after the result is known. Showing the periods that failed next to the one that worked, with the reason they failed, is the version a reader can trust.

Telling what a company does from what it says. Reading an organization from its own documents is the same problem in sustainability reporting, in risk, and in any due-diligence review of a company being bought. The useful evidence is the specific, numbered claim about its own operations. General statements written for an audience add little.

Finding out what the audience rewards before producing for it. Most organizations keep a record of what their audience has rewarded: prior winning entries, prior approved deals, board memos that got a decision. It is almost never read before the work starts.
