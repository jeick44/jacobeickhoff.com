Reading which companies use AI, not which ones talk about it

The Competition: four of us built a score for how far each S&P 500 company had put AI to work inside its own business, read from its earnings calls and filings. Built only from documents through the end of 2025, it ranked the index, and over the first eight weeks of 2026 the top quartile beat the bottom by 11.4%.

The Situation

The one thing a hedge fund case competition wants is a formal paper carrying an investable idea and enough evidence that somebody can argue with it. Early in 2026 nearly every such idea priced who sells AI, meaning the chips, the models and the clouds they run on, and far fewer were about who had actually put it to work.

That gap stays open because the evidence is awkward, since companies say a great deal about AI in their filings and on their earnings calls and most of it is positioning. So the question we took was whether the documents alone separate a company running AI in its operations from a company with an AI paragraph, and whether the market pays for the difference.

Why the paper was about operators rather than sellers

In a winner-take-most market the returns to a technology concentrate in a handful of firms, and the vendors are where that concentration shows first, because their revenue is the adoption. For an operator the same adoption arrives as cost that has stopped being incurred, which surfaces later and through a noisier channel.

The paper’s argument is that the lag is where a public-market reader can still act, because by the time an operator’s advantage shows in its margin it shows to everybody, and the earnings calls and filings move first. What Changed shows how that argument held up in the numbers.

What I Did

The team built a pipeline that reads every earnings call since 2024 and every SEC filing since 2023 for 487 S&P 500 companies. It pulls out each sentence that ties AI to a financial result, such as a cost saved, a margin improved or work done with fewer people, and sorts it into one of 12 operational categories. Claude Opus then scores each of those sentences from 0 to 6, where 0 is buzzwords and 6 is a CFO citing dollar figures across several deployments.

Those sentence scores are joined with Bloomberg figures on margin, SG&A and operating leverage, and a set of four machine learning models turns the lot into one score between 0 and 1 for each company. The models learned from the AI winners and losers that Goldman Sachs and UBS had each published from their own research, so the labels came from outside the team rather than from our own guesses.

My part: I owned the model, from how each sentence was scored through to the ensemble that produced the final number. Nobody on the team stayed inside one section, though. We broke every part of the paper into pieces and passed them around, so I also worked on the financial modeling, the index construction and the presentation, and every piece, the model included, was read by someone who had not written it.

Before any of that, I read every prior year’s winning entry to work out what the judges actually rewarded, and I co-wrote the final submission. My three teammates are not named here, because none of them has approved a public write-up of their work, and putting their names on my portfolio is their decision to make, not mine.

Reading the prior winners before writing a word

The entries that had won in previous years were not the ones with the strongest backtest. They were the ones whose thesis could be stated in a sentence, and whose evidence a judge could check inside the time a judge actually has.

That changed what we produced: fewer exhibits, each defensible on its own, and every figure in the abstract carrying the window it was measured over. It is the most transferable thing I did here, and it was not analysis. It was finding out what the audience rewards before spending six weeks producing for it.

The four things the score measures, and the one thing it caps

First, whether a company’s AI language is about its own operations or about AI it sells. “AI cut our cost per package 8%” is a company using AI, and “our AI platform serves enterprise clients” is a company selling it. Any company whose AI language is more than 70% the selling kind is capped at 0.35, which keeps chipmakers and cloud providers, whose prices already reflect the enthusiasm, out of the top of the ranking.

Second, how often the company puts a number on the result. Sentences with a dollar or percentage outcome count for more than general claims of efficiency. Third, the gap between what management says in its prepared remarks and how hard analysts press on it in the Q&A, since claims that analysts ignore are more likely to be promotion. Fourth, whether the company’s talk about AI is speeding up quarter over quarter, which picks out companies early in deployment before the savings reach the margin.

The paper names its own limits. Banks, insurers and REITs are scored on their documents alone, because the usual margin measures do not fit how they make money. The models learned from only 34 labeled companies, so the paper leans on forward returns as its main evidence rather than on how well the models fit those 34.

What Changed

Measured, and ours to state: the model was built only from documents through the end of 2025, so every 2026 return is one it never saw. Across the 487 companies, the top quartile by score returned 9.9% from the start of January to February 27, 2026, while the bottom quartile lost 1.5%, a spread of 11.4%. The equal-weight S&P 500 returned 3.5% over the same weeks. The pattern held across the whole range of scores and not only at the ends, with a correlation of +0.296 between a company’s score and its 2026 return.

Run against earlier years, the same score pointed the other way. The top quartile trailed the bottom by 18.4% in 2024 and by 11.9% in 2025, and the paper reports both years in full.

Those two years are the mechanism the paper is built on, not a failure it has to explain away. The companies that put AI to work in 2022 and 2023 had already been rewarded by the market before our documents begin, and they said little about AI in 2024 and 2025 because that work was finished, so they scored low while their stock did well. By 2026 a new group was in the middle of deploying, describing AI in its operations in specific terms with the savings not yet in its earnings, and that is the group the score ranks highest.

The financials point the same way. A company’s score has almost no relationship with its change in operating margin over 2024 and 2025 (r = −0.070 across 350 non-financial companies), which is what the paper expects, because firms deploying AI are paying for it now and have not yet banked the savings. The market is paying for them anyway, and that gap is what the strategy trades.

Two further checks back this up. On the 453 companies the models never saw a label for, the Buy tier returned 9.2% against 6.6% for the rest, so the score is picking out the pattern across the index rather than memorising the 34 it learned from. And the Goldman Sachs winners against the UBS losers went from −50.4% in 2024 to +13.9% in 2025 to +32.6% in early 2026, the same arc as the score. The paper is plain that eight weeks is a short live window, and a full year of 2026 returns is the real test.

For anyone reading Engagement 04 next, this model and the pipeline I later withdrew in The Research are separate pieces of work. They measure different things in different documents, operational adoption here and machine authorship there, and this paper was written months before that pipeline existed. I have not compared the two line by line, so that is the whole basis for the claim, but it is enough to say the withdrawal there does not reach back into the result here.

What I Learned

That a result which looks like a failure can turn out to be the evidence, once you ask why it happened.

Why the losing years went into the paper

Leaving 2024 and 2025 out would have made a cleaner paper. But a judge who found the losing years on their own would have found a hole, while a judge handed them along with the reason is reading a paper that already knows where it is weak.

Working out why those years lost is what gave the paper its argument, which is the lag between a company putting AI to work and the savings showing up in its numbers. Without the losing years there would have been nothing to explain, and the 2026 result would have been one good window with no reason behind it.

The second thing is about how a team checks its own work. Because every piece was passed around, nobody was the last reader of their own section. That mattered most for the model, since I owned it and was the person least able to see its gaps.

Where Else This Applies

Reporting the periods that went against you, and explaining them. Any performance claim, whether for a strategy, a pricing change, a marketing channel or a process redesign, is measured over some window, and the window is usually chosen after the result is known. Showing the periods that failed next to the one that worked, with the reason they failed, is the version a reader can trust.

Telling what a company does from what it says. Reading an organization from its own documents is the same problem in sustainability reporting, in risk, and in any diligence read of a target. The useful signal is the specific, numbered claim about its own operations, and the noise is the general statement written for an audience.

Finding out what the audience rewards before producing for it. The record of what an audience has actually rewarded, such as prior winning entries, prior approved deals and prior board memos that got a decision, exists in most organizations and is almost never read before the work starts.
