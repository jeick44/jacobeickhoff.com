Engagement 04 · Independent · faculty-advised

The Research

The question is whether the passages of an SEC filing that a language model has revised earn different abnormal returns around the filing date than the ones a person wrote. I published a first version of this study in June 2026, checked it myself, found its central result appeared at every date I tested rather than only at the one it claimed, and withdrew it. This page is the rebuild — the same question, an instrument built so that it can come back empty, and a pass bar written down before any detector ran.

Pre-registered The go/no-go bar for this study was fixed in a dated file before any detector had been run against the validation set, and nothing below has been revised in light of a number. The work is at the validation stage: the corpus is extracted and the detectors are being measured. No panel result exists yet, and the page says so where one would otherwise go.

What this Engagement produced

  • 4,653 passages extracted

    Located by each filer’s own structured tags, every one traceable back to text a reader can open.

  • 3 edit intensities

    The Detectability Curve: accuracy at light, moderate and heavy revision, rather than one number standing for all three.

  • 1 result withdrawn

    My own published finding, checked by me, retracted with its three defects named and dated.

Section 01

The Situation

Finance research reads corporate disclosure as text — tone, hedging, readability, how much a filing changed since last year. All of it rests on an assumption that used to be free: that a person wrote the words. If a language model revised them, the measure is reading the model’s style, and a company that looks newly cautious may be a company that started running its footnotes through an assistant.

Nobody can say how often that happens, for a narrow reason: no published detector has been validated on financial disclosure. The ones that exist were trained to separate web prose from model output, and a footnote is neither. So the first question is not whether AI-generated language predicts returns. It is whether it can be seen at all in this kind of writing, and that has to be settled against labelled data before a filing is scored.

Why the thing being detected is an edit and not a draft

An issuer does not hand a model a blank page and file what comes back. What a drafter plausibly does is write the paragraph and ask for it to be tightened with the facts kept, so the positive class here is a human passage a model revised rather than one a model produced from nothing. That is what AI-generated language means on this page and in the study: an edit, not a draft. Wholly generated text is kept only as an upper bound: a detector that cannot separate that from human writing is blind, and a null result from a blind instrument says nothing.

Section 02

What I Did

I rebuilt the measurement instrument. It has four parts.

Passages are located by the filer’s own tags. The first version found the notes to the financial statements with a regular expression over the document text. The extractor now reads the index the filer publishes alongside the filing and takes the sections the filer tagged as notes — 4,653 passages from 1,324 notes across 74 filings, drawn from a 40-company sample of which 37 had filings in the window. All of them are dated before 2020, so the human labels are certain by construction.

The labels are built, not assumed. A validation set of 200 human passages, 600 model-revised versions of them, and 50 wholly generated for the upper bound. Every revision is paired with its own original, so a detector can be asked whether it scores a passage as more machine-like than the text it came from.

The instrument reports a Detectability Curve, not a score. Accuracy is measured at three rungs of edit intensity — light, moderate, heavy — and a passage’s rung is measured afterwards from edit distance and n-gram overlap against its original, never inferred from the prompt that asked for it. A prompt is a request; edit intensity is what happened.

The Gate. A detector may score real filings only if it reaches an AUC of at least 0.70 at the moderate rung and holds its false-positive rate at or below 5% against genuine pre-2020 footnotes. Four were named in advance: the detector the withdrawn pipeline scored with, kept as the incumbent baseline; Fast-DetectGPT; Binoculars; and a stylometric floor with no neural weights behind it. If none clears the bar, the study stops at the validation stage and reports that LLM editing of filing footnotes is not detectable with current public methods — and that is the finding, not a failure to find one. The bar is not lowered, the ladder is not re-cut, and the panel is not scored in the hope that scale compensates.

What a pilot run is allowed to decide

Before paying for a strong model to write the edited passages, I ran the pipeline end to end against revisions written by Llama 3.1 8B on my own machine. Against that generator the stylometric floor separated revisions from their own originals at an AUC of 0.781 at the moderate rung, rising at every rung and surviving where the revision lost no length at all. The two neural detectors sat at or below chance on its edited text; against wholly generated text both did better, and one of them almost perfectly.

None of that is a result, and the run file says so on its first line: an 8B model has a strong stylistic signature, a stylometric index measures exactly that, and the pilot may therefore be detecting one model rather than detecting editing. I wrote that down before the paid run rather than after. The one thing it decided is that the paid run is worth making.

Section 03

What Changed

What changed is the thing I would lead with in an interview: I withdrew my own published result. A report I compiled in June 2026 carried three headline findings. I re-examined it against the source data and against live filings, found three defects, and superseded it.

The score was a ranking wearing a probability’s clothes. The final quantity was a within-sample z-score through a sigmoid, and a filing was flagged if it landed in the top quartile — which flags exactly a quarter of any sample by construction, whatever the text says, and means adding one company changes the score of every company already scored. It was reported as though it were the probability that a filing was machine-written.

The extractor was reading the wrong text. Tested against six live filings, five landed on a cross-reference or a table-of-contents line rather than on the notes. One company ranked near the top for AI language had been scored on 4,109 characters of exhibit index and signature page. The heading pattern the extractor searched for matched zero times in all six: a modern filing renders a note heading as “1. Significant Accounting Policies”, never as “Note 1 —”.

The trend appeared at dates where nothing had happened. I re-ran the specification with a fabricated break in November of every year from 2017 to 2024, and every one came back significant. Adding a linear time trend removed the effect, and so did a separate specification carrying firm fixed effects and a form-type control. The score drifts smoothly upward across the decade, and the date I had picked is a point on that line rather than a break in it.

So I wrote a correction, dated it, said the report was superseded and should not be cited, and recorded that it go to anyone who had received it. The pipeline was then rebuilt from scratch, with a provenance record on every passage — accession number, source URL, note, character offsets. Any number in the write-up now traces back to text a reader can open, which is the discipline whose absence let a signature page be reported as a finding.

Pending Result Whether AI-generated language in filings predicts abnormal returns is not answered here. The panel is not scored and is not authorised to be scored until a detector clears the Gate. If none does, the reportable finding is non-detectability, with the curve attached so that “filers are not doing this” stays distinguishable from “this instrument cannot see it”.

Where the rest of it stands. The study is faculty-advised, and that is a working relationship rather than a name on a cover page. An application for research funding has been submitted; it has not been decided and it has not been awarded. A conference is the intended venue, whichever way the Gate falls. None of the three is an outcome.

Section 04

What I Learned

That the defects were not carelessness, which is the uncomfortable part. Each one was a choice — which break date, which heading pattern, which cut-off counted as high — made with the answer already on the screen, and none was recorded as a choice because at the time none of them felt like one.

So the remedy is not more care: care is what produced it, aimed in the direction of the result I expected. It is to commit to the decisions before the numbers exist, in a file with a date on it — which is why The Gate can disqualify a detector I would rather keep. The narrower version of the lesson: a result that has survived no placebo is not a result. The check that cost me the finding took an afternoon, and I had not run it because the finding agreed with what I expected.

The case for publishing a correction rather than quietly replacing the work

The tempting version is to rebuild, publish the better study, and let the first one disappear. Nobody had cited it; it would have cost nothing visible. What decided it is that I cannot be sure where the report went, and deleting my copy leaves the claim alive in anyone else’s with nothing attached to it. Naming the three defects is also the only thing that lets a reader judge the rebuild — without them it is a set of answers to no stated question.

Section 05

Where Else This Applies

A score defined by a ranking is not a measurement. Vendor risk tiers, performance bands, lead scores, accounts flagged for review — anything defined as the top quartile identifies that share of the population whatever the population does, and adding a case changes the score of every case already in it. The damage comes when a sorting rule is escalated as a probability.

Check what the extractor actually read. Any process pulling fields out of documents — diligence, compliance review, invoice and contract automation — reports what it found and almost never what it read instead. The cheapest check in this study was opening six documents and looking at the characters that had been scored.

Fix the pass bar before the measurement. Pilot evaluations, model selection and vendor bake-offs reach the same fork: the success criteria are written down before the numbers arrive, or afterwards, in which case they are a description of the numbers. Fixing it first is what makes a negative result reportable rather than embarrassing, and a negative result nobody can report is a budget that keeps getting spent.
