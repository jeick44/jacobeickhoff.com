An allocation process that can be replayed, argued with, and shown to be wrong

MacroSense: whether the disciplined, repeatable allocation process an investment committee is supposed to run can be built and operated end to end by one person, on data anybody can look up. This page is the architecture, and deliberately not a performance claim; the third section says why.

The Situation

Allocation decisions made by hand share a defect that has nothing to do with whether they are any good: they are made once, defended in a meeting, and never recorded in a form anybody can re-run. Six months later there is no process to interrogate — there was a sequence of opinions.

A system that executes on a schedule, from stated inputs, and writes down what it did and why is a different object. It can be replayed, argued with on its merits, and — the part that matters — shown to be wrong. So the question was not whether I could pick sectors, but whether the whole loop could be built and run by one person, with no step that works only because somebody is watching.

One constraint was fixed at the start and never relaxed. Every input is free and public: Treasury yields and credit spreads from the Federal Reserve’s published series, volatility and fund prices from public market data, and company commentary from 10-K and 10-Q filings and earnings-call transcripts.

Why free and public was a design constraint rather than a budget

The cheap reading is that a student cannot afford a terminal. The real one is that a system built on a subscription cannot be reproduced by a reader or checked by somebody who doubts it, and a process whose whole claim is that it repeats should not have a paywall in the middle of it. The cost is that free sources go missing: identifiers get retired, endpoints change shape, requests come back empty. So every signal resolves through a fallback chain rather than a single call.

What I Did

I built it as separable components, each taking a stated input, returning a stated output, and runnable on its own.

The data fetcher. In: public macro series and public market prices — Treasury yields at two and ten years and the spread between them, high-yield and investment-grade credit spreads (the extra yield corporate bonds pay over Treasuries), the volatility index and its term structure (how expected volatility differs between the next month and the next three), and one- and three-month momentum (recent price return) for each of fifteen sector and credit exchange-traded funds. Out: one dated row, fifty-eight columns wide.

The regime classifier. In: a rolling window of at least twenty of those rows, plus sentiment signals for the energy and healthcare sectors. Out: one of four regimes — Calm Growth, Turbulent Growth, Rate Stress, Risk Off — with a probability for each. The model is a four-state Gaussian hidden Markov model: it treats the market’s regime as something that can’t be seen directly and works out which of four states most likely produced the data. The probabilities matter more than the label: 0.41 against a runner-up at 0.39 says something different from 0.90, so the briefing gets all four, not just the winner.

The portfolio optimizer. In: the regime and the current signal row. Out: weights across the same fifteen funds, summing to one. Each regime changes the constraints (rules the weights must obey), not the model: long only with a cap per position in the two growth regimes; short positions allowed within tighter bounds in the two stress regimes, defensive holdings favored and rate-sensitive ones penalized. A convex solver (a method that finds the single best set of weights under those rules) does the work; when it fails, the optimizer falls back to equal weights and logs the reason as an error.

The sentiment scorer. In: earnings-call transcripts and 10-K and 10-Q filings from the SEC’s public archive, collected by a companion updater that waits between requests to stay under the SEC’s rate limit. Out: a score for each company on four dimensions — revenue tone, cost and margin direction, confident versus hedged language, newly disclosed risk — a weighted composite of the four, and a sector roll-up that goes back to the classifier as one of its features (the inputs a model learns from).

The scheduler. In: the clock and the exchange calendar. Out: a completed cycle, logged. Three jobs — a rebalance after each trading day’s close, a model retrain on the first of the month, a filings pull on Sunday. Every step is fault-tolerant on its own (one can fail without stopping the rest), because a scheduler is only worth having if it’s still running in March.

The briefing. In: the regime name, the probability vector, the previous weights, the new weights, the sentiment signals. Out: a few sentences of plain English saying what changed and why. A language model writes them; with no API key available it falls back to a fixed template, so the record has no gaps on days an outside service was unreachable.

The dashboard. In: the regime and its four probabilities, the weights, the sentiment scores, and every past rebalance, which is saved to a local database. Out: one page showing where the market stands, what the portfolio holds and why, and how both have changed over time.

The rule that kept the components separable

A short list of interfaces was written down and then treated as fixed: what the classifier is called with and what it hands back, that the optimizer returns weights keyed by ticker summing to one, the five arguments the briefing takes. Everything behind those signatures was rewritten repeatedly — the fetcher most of all — and none of those rewrites reached another module. Where the shapes are not fixed, every change is a whole-system change, and the symptom is that you stop making improvements because you cannot price them.

What Changed

The system works end to end. It fires on schedule, fetches, classifies, allocates, explains itself and logs the result, and it has been doing that rather than being demonstrated once. That is what I set out to build, and it is done.

The backtest harness is built too. It reuses the live classifier and the live optimizer verbatim rather than reimplementing the strategy, because a backtest that reimplements the thing it is testing is testing the reimplementation. It walks the history forward one rebalance at a time, classifying on data up to that date and no further, applies real daily fund returns afterwards, charges a cost against turnover, and compares the result with buying and holding the broad market. It computes what a reader would ask for: total return, a compound growth rate, a Sharpe ratio, volatility, maximum drawdown, attribution by regime, and a distribution of outcomes.

None of those numbers appears on this page.

Pending Result Whether MacroSense allocates well is not answered here. The continuous history it has accumulated is fifty-six trading days, from 7 January to 27 March 2026. Replayed at the backtest’s default weekly rebalance that is roughly eleven decisions; monthly, two. Every ratio in the list above would be annualized out of that, a single market environment three months long, and a figure produced that way describes the window rather than the system. Backfilling the snapshot series so the model can be run across several years and several regimes is the current work. The numbers go on this page when the window can carry them, and not before.

One thing belongs alongside that, because it is the same error one level down: the history carries no scored sentiment at all — those columns are absent from it, and the classifier imputes them to zero — so the company layer has never been in a backtest. The macro layer has.

What I Learned

The first time the backtest rendered an equity curve next to its benchmark, the shape of it felt like a result. It was not one, and the distance between those two things is what building this actually taught me.

Sample size is not a caveat you attach at the end; it decides whether a number is a number at all. Fifty-six daily observations look like a sample and are closer to one — a single market environment, read fifty-six times. The ratios a backtest prints are annualized, which makes that window wear the clothes of a year, and nothing in the output says so.

The harder half is that a system which runs is enormously satisfying, and the satisfaction is misleading. Every day the scheduler fires, a cycle completes, a briefing is written and a row lands in the database — and none of it is evidence about whether the allocations are any good. Running and being right are unrelated properties, and only one of them gives feedback.

What it would take for a number to go on this page

A history long enough to contain all four regimes, with enough time in each that the per-regime constraint sets have been exercised against real returns. Scored sentiment across it instead of placeholders. And the result reported with its window, its costs and the periods where it went the other way — the standard Engagement 03 is held to on this site.

Where Else This Applies

A pipeline that runs is not a pipeline that is right. Any automated process handed to a client — a nightly reporting refresh, a scoring model, a forecast that regenerates itself — produces its most convincing evidence, a clean run, on the one axis nobody was asking about. The question to put to a working system is not whether it ran. It is what would have to be true for the output to be wrong, and whether anybody has gone and looked.

State the window in the same breath as the figure. A pilot over six weeks, a test over two, a model checked against one quarter: each produces a point estimate whose uncertainty is wide enough to contain the opposite conclusion, and each is routinely escalated as a finding. The discipline costs nothing — the window travels with the number, and nothing is annualized out of a window that cannot support it.

Make the degraded path announce itself. Two defects here were the same defect: an optimizer that fell back to equal weight when its import failed, and an alert channel built but never configured, which returned quietly every time. Both kept producing output after a component had stopped working, which is worse than stopping, because nothing downstream can tell. Any monitoring or integration work meets this in some form, and the fallback that never announces itself costs the most.
