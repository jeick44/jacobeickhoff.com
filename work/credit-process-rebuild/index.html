Rebuilding loan origination as tools that never touch a server

Credit Process Rebuild: a ground-up rework of how a loan request reaches the credit committee at a community bank in the Upper Midwest, replacing a chain of undocumented spreadsheets with nine browser tools that never send customer data to a server. I lead it as Credit Analyst & AI Implementation Lead, embedded with the credit team and doing the analyst work alongside the build.

The Situation

This project started with the customer map. That work got me in the door at the bank, and afterward the CFO asked if I wanted to spend the summer working on processes he thought needed improving, mostly to see what I could do with them. Once my internship started, the loan process was the first thing I saw that could be done better.

When a lender submits a request for a new loan, a renewal or a modification, an analyst turns it into the approval presentation, which is the document the credit committee makes its decision from. Everything between those two points ran through a legacy Excel system on a shared drive. The credit request workbook fed three more files: the presentation figures, a written description of the deal and the spreads, which lay out the borrower’s financials year by year. Each file was built separately and pulled in its own direction, none of the logic was written down, and a single concept could carry two different definitions in the same workbook without anything flagging it. A presentation took a lot of analyst time to build and still came out inconsistent.

The Local-Only Constraint was in place before any design work began, meaning that no customer data reaches a cloud service and no tool requires a server. I treated it as the starting point of the project rather than an obstacle, because the reasoning behind it is a threat model, an account of what an attacker could reach, rather than an IT preference. The planned hub keeps to it by running on the bank’s own server, which never faces outside the bank.

Why the server stays inside the bank

Today a compromised workstation yields only what that analyst could already open from the shared drive, but it also takes the work open on it down with it, since nothing in progress is saved anywhere else. With the planned server inside the bank, the compromised machine is cut off, no other machine is touched, and anything in progress is already saved.

The cost is concentration. The server’s service account has to read every file, so it bypasses the shared drive’s permissions by design, and a compromise of the server itself would yield every customer. That makes it the one machine worth hardening, and it is why the server sits only on the bank’s own network and never faces outside it.

A server also turns a document on the shared drive into a formal internal service, which at a regulated institution means risk assessment, sign-off and examiner scrutiny before it goes live.

What I Did

I started with a current-state assessment of credit origination, mapping where the process broke down across intake, documentation and risk presentation, and then laid out a phased delivery roadmap. Alongside building the tools, I continued doing standard analyst work, assessing credit risk and spreading financials, which kept the design grounded in how the work actually gets done.

What started as a simple project turned out to have a lot of nuance, so I took the whole process apart and rebuilt it from the ground up, with the goal of combining four separate documents into one fluid form. Not everyone was sold on that at first. The CEO’s view was that the Excel files worked, and that code would be more likely to break than a spreadsheet. What brought him around was a roadmap and an early rendering of the layout, which showed exactly what the bank would get before any of the files were replaced. The CFO backed the project the whole way through, and the analysts and lenders were kept in the loop as it moved forward.

The system is made up of nine browser tools, and that number continues to grow as the project expands. Each tool is opened directly from the shared drive, so there is no online server, no network connection and no database beyond the bank’s own private data. Together the tools let analysts work with client information more effectively than they could before, saving time and cutting down on the places where errors can enter. The main security control is that the data never leaves the bank’s machines, and the goal was to make internal data feel as usable and automated as an external service would without giving up that control. An automated check enforces this by blocking any release in which a shipped file makes a network call or loads anything from outside.

Buying a vendor platform was the obvious alternative, but a small bank needs more flexibility than most packaged systems allow, so the bank chose tools built around its own data and process instead. I built them on my own, using AI for the coding and drawing on the MIT Sloan course Artificial Intelligence: Implications for Business Strategy. Most of the build happened in 40-plus-hour weeks over the summer, and the work has continued alongside school since.

Two pieces of the rebuild are process design, and the first is the Three-Stage Review. The approval presentation used to be produced by working toward three objectives at once: filling in the figures, working out why they look the way they do, and checking the result. Every question the analyst could not answer stopped the analysis for a call to the lender who wrote the deal, which made for a choppy process with numerous delays. The Three-Stage Review separates the work into filling the figures, analyzing the why and then reviewing, so the questions only the lender can answer are no longer a bottleneck and are instead batched into a single step at the end that finalizes the file.

How lender questions are batched, and why

Each lender question is marked where it arises, so none is lost before the call.

The cost being cut is the lender’s time, which is the scarcer resource. A change that saves the analyst an hour but costs the lender two is a net loss.

The second tool is built to remove a manual process entirely by reading tax documents. It takes a business tax return and produces an income statement for that customer, along with debt-service coverage, which is the cash available to pay debt divided by the debt payments due. The rule built into the tool is that a wrong number costs more than a missing one, so a missing number shows up as visibly missing and a number the tool cannot trust is flagged rather than passed through.

That is why the tool refuses a scanned, image-only return (a picture of each page with no text to read) and explains why, rather than leaving the user to guess what caused the error. A line whose description is scrambled is held for review, and the reading step sits behind a pluggable interface so a vendor-approved method can drop in later.

What the tool does with a return it cannot read

The refusal names the problem and points the analyst to the route that works. Character recognition could turn the image into numbers, but they would only look like figures. A scrambled line keeps its amount, flagged for review, and is never attached to the nearest plausible label.

The project is now in the AI Integration Phase, starting with a prototype policy assistant and an AI-enabled tax scanner, both pending compliance review. The next step is a single hub where every tool can be opened from one place and each one can feed the others. Each tool still runs without it, but the hub is being built so it can sit on the bank’s own internal server and nowhere outside it.

What Changed

In my own use, a request that used to take days now takes hours, and by my estimate roughly 90% of the repeatable work is now automated, so analysts spend their time on the judgment calls that actually need them. Every analyst on the team now has access to the tools.

Nine tools have shipped, and 3,923 automated checks cover the approval-presentation tool alone. A second program, the fault injector, plants the faults those checks exist to catch and confirms that each check fails when it should. Nine architecture decisions are also documented, each alongside the alternative it was chosen over.

Pending Results Lender callbacks per file and the rate of corrected figures in a finished approval presentation do not yet have a before-and-after measurement I would stand behind, so that data is being collected now and will be added once it is complete.

What I Learned

A test suite that passes tells you the tests passed and nothing else, which I learned from a single change to the spreads import. The change generalized the import so that a new kind of financial tab could reuse the code the existing tabs already relied on, and it worked, with every check passing. A code review of that same change still found six defects, and two of them would have corrupted figures on their way into a credit decision.

The project also taught me that a faster process is only as effective as its ability to clear the bottleneck, which is the core idea of Eliyahu M. Goldratt’s The Goal. Here the bottleneck was the lender’s time, and speeding up the analysis without batching the callbacks would only have moved the work faster toward a closed door.

The two that would have reached real figures

The check that decides what kind of sheet the tool is looking at read the whole sheet instead of just the header rows. To it, a block of round four-figure expense amounts looks like a row of years. A real sheet would have been classified wrongly and handed to a parser that sorts columns by year. Two properties’ figures would then land under one year, one silently overwriting the other. The survivor would merge into that company’s real multi-year trend.

The second was in the data migration, which moves old data into the new format. Before deleting the original, it checked the copy by comparing the data with itself. That comparison could not fail, and it was the only safeguard before the analyst’s hand-entered grid was deleted.

What matters more than the six defects themselves is that the checks, the fault injector and I were all satisfied, and reading the change as prose was the only thing left that caught them. I write the tests, and I still need the review.

Where Else This Applies

Learning why a constraint exists before designing around it. Healthcare, legal and defense work all run under rules that outsiders read as obstruction, but those rules often protect something real, and knowing which is which is the difference between negotiating a constraint and building within it.

Batching questions for the person whose time is scarce. Any workflow where one role waits on another’s answers has this problem, so a redesign should be judged by the total interruptions across everyone involved, not by the hours saved for whoever designed it.

The refusal rule. Any process that turns documents such as claims, invoices, applications and filings into figures someone makes decisions from has to choose between producing a number and admitting it cannot, because a system that guesses reads exactly like one that knows until someone checks an output that has already been acted on.
