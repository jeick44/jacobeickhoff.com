The Situation

Two questions came up often enough that they needed a real answer. Where are our customers, and what does our age profile look like? The core system exported rows showing an address, a balance and sometimes a date of birth, and rows are not a map but static numbers that say little on their own. Turning them into a useful picture meant hours of manual work in a spreadsheet, for an answer that might not be worth it.

That cost never shows up as a problem, which is exactly why it matters. When a question is expensive to answer, people stop asking it, often without realizing they should or knowing how, and nobody keeps a record of the questions that never got asked.

What an expensive question costs, and why nobody logs it

A question that takes a day to answer does not get asked often. It gets asked only when somebody is already willing to spend the day finding out. Sometimes people do not know how to ask the question at all, so they cannot picture what the answer might be. The first thing these tools changed was the price of asking.

What I Did

I built four browser tools, three maps and a data-preparation utility. One map shows where customers are and who they are, one shows what each location earns, and one compares two periods, while the utility turns a raw export into the one format all three read. Each is a single HTML file opened on the computer it runs on, with no server, no login, no database and nothing to install, so nothing needed approval before someone could try it. The only thing any of them downloads is map imagery, and no customer data leaves the computer. I built them in a deliberate order, starting with a version that only had to prove the idea could work.

I also built a prototype that puts an assistant on top of the same data. It runs a trained language model, Llama, inside the browser, so a user can ask a question in plain words, go back and forth until it is the right question, or use the map as before, and the customer data still never leaves the computer.

From a proof of concept to an interaction model

The first version was a map with two layers and dots at hardcoded coordinates, about five hundred lines of code with no real data in it. Its only job was to prove this could be done in a browser.

Everything about this project was new to me, so before building further I met with someone who has years of experience in GIS to ask what good practice looks like. He pushed back on my format. Points on a map that do nothing say nothing. That changed how I thought about it, and led to a map that calculates as you zoom in and shades each area by the income it earns.

The idea that turned the picture into a tool was simple. Select an area and see real numbers for it. Click to drop a circle, drag a slider to size it, and the panel reports what is inside, including the dollars, the record counts and the age distribution. Everything added afterwards applies that idea to something else. It also changed the demos. A map of dots gets looked at, and a number for the spot somebody pointed at gets argued with.

A color is a conclusion, so the location shows its arithmetic

The profitability map colors each location by its net annual income, which is what its loans earn at their own rates minus what its deposits cost. The scale runs from green to red, and a grid view groups an area into cells the reader can size. But a color is a conclusion presented as an observation, and a reader has no way to check it. So hovering a location does not repeat its color. It shows the subtraction, loan income minus deposit expense, and the net that results.

Each line is also the line a lender would challenge first, so any disagreement happens over a rate or a balance, where it can be settled.

This is the original layout, the version I first presented to the CEO, COO, CFO and the board. It shows where customers are and what each area earns, which gave leadership a clearer view of its customer base than any export could.

Detect the anomaly, correct only when it is safe, flag the correction visibly

Real exports arrive broken in small ways that affect the outputs, such as a title row above the headers, an address with no coordinates or an age column holding dates. One tool handles all of it by one standing rule.

Detect the problem, and correct it only when the correction is unambiguous. A date of birth becomes an age, and a missing coordinate is filled in from a matching address in a file that was already cleaned. Otherwise the cell is left empty. Every correction is flagged where the person using the output cannot miss it. The cell is highlighted, the reason shows on hover, and a count of corrections sits above the preview. A tool that quietly improves its data looks exactly like one that quietly damages it.

What Changed

The tools are in active use by the bank’s CEO, CFO and senior lending staff, and I presented them formally to the CEO, COO, CFO and the board. The outcome I am most confident in came as a byproduct of the work. This Engagement is why the bank offered me an ongoing role, and the quality of these tools is why I have kept building more of them there.

The tools also turned up something nobody had seen before. The bank’s deposits are concentrated in its oldest customers, enough that it belongs in a conversation about funding rather than demographics. It is described here without its figures on purpose. The tools are my work product and the finding is the Sponsor’s business, so only one of them gets a number on this site.

What I Learned

That a check which returns a plausible answer on bad input is worse than one that refuses.

An age column sometimes arrives holding date serials, which are dates of birth stored as a count of days and never formatted as dates. The map recognized these and converted them, but only above a floor of twenty thousand days, which lands in October 1954. So any customer born earlier, roughly anyone seventy-one or older, failed the serial test, then every other test, and came back as zero. Zero is a real age band in the tool, not an error value, so those customers were neither dropped nor flagged. They were filed with the infants, and the band being emptied was the very band the demographic finding was about. It was the third defect of that kind here, after an age filter that counted records it had been told to exclude and a loader that read every dollar value as zero. It taught me to check what goes into a tool as carefully as what comes out, and to keep a record of every change made to the data along the way.

How it surfaced, and how it was fixed

I did not find it by reading the code but by building a fixture from the shape of a real export to test something else, and two of its ten ages came back as zero when I knew what they should have been. The same check had been written twice a few weeks apart and the two copies had drifted, while the preparation tool had the range right the whole time.

The fix checks for a plausible age first and a date serial second, and any value that fits neither is now flagged instead of being filed at zero. Due to the fact it moved ages on screen in a tool executives were already reading, I told them before it went in, since numbers changing without warning is its own problem even when they change from wrong to right.

The general lesson, and one I have found no way around, is that real files break parsers in ways no synthetic fixture predicts, because synthetic data is built on the same assumptions the parser was written under and so agrees with the code by design. Every edge case the preparation tool handles came from a file that broke it first.

Where Else This Applies

Pricing a question before answering it. Any organization where an ordinary question costs a day of somebody’s time has a set of questions it has stopped asking and no record of which ones.

Making a visual show its arithmetic. Heat maps, scores, ratings, risk tiers and red-amber-green dashboards all hand the reader a conclusion with the working removed, and each is believed until the first person checks one.

Detect, correct only when safe, flag always. Any pipeline that cleans data on its way into a decision, whether claims intake, survey responses, meter reads or a system migration, has to choose between staying silent about what it changed and showing it.
