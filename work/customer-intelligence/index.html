The Situation

Two questions came up often enough to be recognisable: where are our customers, and what does our age profile look like. Both were answerable; neither was answerable cheaply. The core system exported rows — an address, a balance, sometimes a date of birth — and rows are not a map. Either view meant hours of manual work in a spreadsheet, so the answer arrived after the conversation that wanted it, or not at all.

The cost is the part worth noticing, because it never appears as a problem. Nobody records the analysis they decided not to ask for.

What an expensive question costs, and why nobody logs it

A question that takes a day to answer is not a question with a one-day turnaround. It is a question asked only when somebody is already willing to spend the day — rarely, and usually once the answer can only confirm. So the backlog is invisible: no queue of unasked questions, no report of the analysis nobody requested. What these tools changed first was the price of asking.

What I Did

Four single-file browser tools: three maps — one descriptive, one financial, one comparing two periods — and a data-preparation utility that turns a raw export into the one shape all three read. Each is a single HTML file opened from the machine it runs on: no server, no login, no database, nothing to install, and so nothing to approve before it could be tried. The one thing any of them fetches is map imagery; no customer data leaves the machine. The order they arrived in is the part I would defend.

From a proof of concept to an interaction model

The first version held no real data: a map, dots at hardcoded coordinates, two layers, about five hundred lines. Its job was to settle the question that would otherwise have blocked every other one — whether this could be done in a browser, with no backend, here.

What turned the picture into a tool was one idea: select an area, see real numbers for it. Click to drop a circle, drag a slider to size it, and the panel reports what is inside — dollars, record counts, the age distribution. Everything added afterwards is that idea on another dimension. It also changed the demos: a map of dots gets looked at, and a number that answers for the spot somebody pointed at gets argued with.

A colour is a conclusion, so the location shows its arithmetic

The profitability map colours each location by its net annual income — what its loans earn at their own rate, less what its deposits cost — green through red, with a grid view that aggregates an area into cells the reader sizes. But a colour scale is a conclusion presented as an observation, and a reader has no way to check one, so hovering a location does not restate its colour. It shows the subtraction: loan income, less deposit expense, is the net.

Each line is also the line a lender would challenge first, so the disagreement happens over a rate or a balance, where it can be settled.

Detect the anomaly, correct only when it is safe, flag the correction visibly

Real exports arrive broken in small ways: a title row above the headers, an address with no coordinates, an age column holding dates. One tool absorbs all of it, to one standing rule.

Detect the anomaly. Correct it only where the correction is unambiguous — a date of birth becomes an age, a missing coordinate is filled from a matching address in a previously cleaned file. Otherwise leave the cell empty. And flag every correction where the person using the output cannot miss it: the cell highlighted, its reason on hover, a count of them above the preview. A tool that quietly improves its input is indistinguishable from one that quietly damages it.

What Changed

The tools are in active use by the bank’s CFO, its president and its senior lending staff, and were the subject of a formal presentation to that group. The outcome I am most confident stating is the one I did not compute: this Engagement is why I was offered an ongoing role, which was somebody else’s assessment of the work rather than mine.

They also produced a finding nobody had stated before: a concentration of the deposit base in the bank’s oldest customers, pronounced enough to belong in a funding conversation rather than a demographic one. It is described here without its figures, and that is not modesty. The tools are my work product and the finding is the Sponsor’s business; only one of those gets a number on this site.

What I Learned

That a check which returns a plausible answer on bad input is worse than one that refuses.

An age column sometimes arrives holding date serials — a date of birth stored as a count of days, never formatted as a date. The map recognised that and converted it, above a floor of twenty thousand days, which lands in October 1954. So a customer born earlier — anyone aged roughly seventy-one or older — failed the serial test, then every other test, and came back as zero. Zero is a real age band in the tool rather than an error value, so those customers were neither dropped nor flagged. They were filed with the infants, and the band being emptied was the band the demographic finding was about. It was the third defect of that kind here: an age filter that counted records it had been told to exclude, a loader that read every dollar value as zero, and this.

How it surfaced, and why it is still logged rather than fixed

Not by reading the code. I built a fixture from the shape of a real export to test something else, and two of its ten ages came back as zero when I knew what they should have been. The same guard, written twice a few weeks apart, had drifted; the preparation tool had the range right the whole time.

It is logged with its fix rather than quietly corrected, because applying it moves ages on screen in a tool executives are reading now — and from wrong to right is still somebody’s numbers changing without warning.

The general lesson is the one I have found no way around. Real files break parsers in ways no synthetic fixture predicts, because synthetic data is generated out of the same assumptions the parser was written under: it agrees with the code by construction. Every edge case the preparation tool handles came from a file that broke it first.

Where Else This Applies

Pricing a question before answering it. Any organisation where an ordinary question costs a day of somebody’s time has a set of questions it has stopped asking and no record of which ones.

Making a visual show its arithmetic. Heat maps, scores, ratings, risk tiers, red-amber-green dashboards — each hands a reader a conclusion with the working removed, and each is believed until the first person checks one.

Detect, correct only when safe, flag always. Any pipeline cleaning data on its way into a decision — claims intake, survey responses, meter reads, a system migration — chooses between silence and visibility about what it changed.
